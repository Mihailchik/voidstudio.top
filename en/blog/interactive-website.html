Interactive websites: what they are, with examples

An interactive website responds to what a person does: it changes with the mouse, a touch, a gesture or a sound. Here are examples that open from a link.

What an interactive website is

An ordinary site shows finished text and pictures. An interactive site does something in response: it calculates, draws, moves, gives a result. The person on it is a participant, not a reader.

A site like this gives people a reason to share it: everyone gets a result of their own.

Kinds of interactive websites

  • A web service. It gathers data and gives a ready answer to a question.
  • Interactive graphics. An image the visitor controls.
  • A browser game. It opens from a link with nothing to install.
  • Unusual navigation. The way you move through the site becomes part of it.

Four examples

Inkfall: ink in water

Inkfall is water with ink blooming through it. You can drive it with a mouse, several fingers at once, hand gestures in front of the camera and your voice through the microphone. The fluid motion is computed from real equations on the GPU, which is why the ink behaves as if it were alive. There are six modes, from heavy oil to light smoke. The camera image is processed on the device and never uploaded.

Surf: is it worth going

Surf is an example of a web service. It gathers the wave, wind and tide forecast for Phuket's beaches from several weather models and turns it into one score from 0 to 10. Instead of a dozen tabs with charts a person gets one answer.

Eyeball: a game for once a day

Eyeball is a browser game with five tasks done by eye: stop a timer blind, find the centre of a circle, set an angle. The tasks are new every day and the same for everyone, so you can compare results with friends. The game has no server; progress is kept in the browser.

The strip of work on our home page

The Void Studio home page is a strip of images you drag with a mouse or a finger. It is drawn on the GPU, and where that is not supported ordinary images are shown.

What interactive websites are built with

  • WebGL — drawing on the GPU right in the browser. Needed for complex graphics and physics.
  • SVG and Canvas — for simpler graphics: diagrams, charts, small games.
  • Camera and microphone — a browser can recognise gestures and sound if the visitor allows it.
  • Open data — weather, maps, exchange rates, timetables.

Nothing has to be installed in any of these cases: everything opens from a link on a phone and on a computer.

What to think about in advance

  • Speed. The site has to open quickly on a phone with a slow connection.
  • A fallback. On an old device the graphics may not start, and the person should still see a simple version.
  • A clear start. A visitor should understand within a few seconds what can be done here.
  • Text for search. A search engine cannot see what is drawn. The essentials have to be repeated as ordinary text.

When interactivity is the right choice

It is justified when it helps people understand a product or get an answer faster than text would. If the job of the site is to give an address and prices, an ordinary page does it better.
